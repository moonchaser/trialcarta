Methodology

Does the Financing Screen find the next royalty deals?

We ran the screen as it would have run on two past dates, using only what was public then, and checked which of the ranked companies went on to disclose a royalty or revenue-interest sale in their SEC filings over the next 30 months. Here is what it found, including what didn't work and what the test can't tell you.

How the test works

  1. Run the screen as of a past date. Industry phase 2 and 3 drug trials on ClinicalTrials.gov with a primary completion 6 to 24 months after that date, sponsors matched to SEC filers, and cash, burn and runway from the financial statements filed by then. Companies with more than 36 months of runway are left out.
  2. Score them with fixed rules. The same points the app uses today: a phase 3 lead trial, 6 to 24 months of runway, a readout within 15 months, 200 or more participants, earlier trials of the lead drug and posted results, the sponsor's record of finishing trials, and a deduction when filings already mention a royalty deal. No AI model is involved in the score.
  3. Leave out companies that had already sold a royalty. Their filings mentioned a royalty deal before the test date, so finding them again would prove nothing.
  4. Look for deals over the next 30 months. Full-text search of every 8-K, 10-Q and 10-K on SEC EDGAR for "royalty purchase agreement", "revenue interest purchase agreement", "royalty interest purchase agreement", "synthetic royalty" and "royalty financing agreement".
  5. Measure the ranking. How many deal companies landed in the top 10, 20 and 50, and the AUC across the whole list.

What this says: among companies with a readout coming and limited runway, the ones that went on to sell a royalty were those with stronger evidence: a phase 3 drug with results already posted, larger trials, and a sponsor that finishes what it starts. Running short of cash before the readout did not, on its own, make a sale more likely, and going-concern wording made it less likely. The default score still gives points for 6 to 24 months of runway and a near readout, because that's when a company has to raise. A score that dropped them did as well on 2021 but no better (AUC 0.81), so they stay.

What the test can't tell you

  • Small numbers. 8 and 12 companies sold a royalty. A difference of one or two companies is noise, and the error bars on each AUC are wide.
  • The default score was chosen after both tests. It's the one that did best on both dates, so these results are not a clean out-of-sample test of it. A third score tuned on the 2022 date didn't beat it on 2021 (AUC 0.81). The fair test is the next 30 months.
  • Today's registry records. ClinicalTrials.gov doesn't keep easy snapshots, so a readout date that slipped after the test date shows its new date, and trials are picked by today's dates.
  • Late filings. Figures for the last quarter before the test date may have been filed shortly after it.
  • Deal wording isn't always a sale. A company can describe a partner's royalty deal in its filings. Each match was listed with its filings so it can be checked.
  • It tests who sells a royalty, not what one is worth. The screen finds companies likely to raise this way. It doesn't value a royalty, estimate a probability of success or size a market. Deal terms are quoted from filings as written, and terms a company redacted aren't filled in. TrialCarta is a research aid, not investment advice.
